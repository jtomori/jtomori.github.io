**Efficient multi view data storage** [ <- ](..) _2025-09-02_ Working with multi-view data, such as volumetric video, is expensive, slow and demanding on storage. In this post I'll explore ways of significantly improving that. # TL;DR The following process can achieve a compression ratio of **18:1** on a typical volumetric video source footage. This can result in significant savings on the required hardware (or cloud storage costs) and increased processing throughput. This process is lossless in the areas we care about - the subject of our capture - and transparent to downstream applications. It can turn this dataset (39 cameras, 3 frames, 12 megapixels, raw DNG, **1 719 MiB** in total): ![](images/sheet_in_censored_lq.jpg width="500px") Into this dataset (raw compressed & segmented DNG, **92 MiB** in total): ![](images/sheet_out_censored_lq.jpg width="500px") Both datasets will result in the same resulting volumetric video, but the compressed one will be much easier on storage, but also will be faster and cheaper to process. !!! note *The multi-view dataset courtesy of [IO Industries](https://www.ioindustries.com/), used with permission, the face has been blurred for privacy reasons.* # What is multi-view data and what is so challenging about it Many applications require multi-view data. In cases of photogrammetry, or volumetric video it's a series of images captured in a single or multiple instances in time respectively. Such data is useful for reconstructing a 3D model (which could be 3D printed, or used as a digital asset in games or visual effects), or generating a new viewpoint (which can be useful for volumetric video and various immersive experiences). Capturing such multi-view data often requires specialized setups and the sheer amount of recorded data (often captured in the highest possible resolution, quality) is often causing a bottleneck in the processing pipeline. Teams storing and processing such data require expensive storage hardware, fast infrastructure requiring a large capital expense on one hand and a slow processing of the data contributes to the operating expenses, especially in case of cloud storage and processing. ![An example dataset, a second of a such data would take 13.4 GiB and a minute would take 806 GiB](images/sheet_in_censored_lq.jpg width="300px") # Workflow The key findings from this research are that the [DNG specification](https://helpx.adobe.com/content/dam/help/en/photoshop/pdf/DNG_Spec_1_7_1_0.pdf) allows for various lossless compressions, which alone offer significant savings (with a compression ratio of around 2:1). Another observation is that the background area of the captured frame takes most of the storage space, while not contributing to the result (and often being discarded early in the reconstruction process). The combination of these two ideas leads to the order of magnitude storage reduction. Now that we understand what does a multi-view data looks like and how much storage it requires, let's take a closer look at each of the proposed optimizations. ## Debayering While both uncompressed and compressed image frames contain raw data (Colour Filter Array or CFA samples), which is basically a monochromatic image - we need a temporary colour image for accurate segmentation. This temporary colour image will feed into the following segmentation step. Since the colour image will be discarded, we can perform debayering at a half resolution and with relatively low quality - both of these decisions do not degrade segmentation quality and decrease the computation time needed for the debayering process. ![Raw (CFA) samples](images/bayered.png width="340px") ![Debayered RGB pixels](images/debayered.png width="340px") ## Segmenting Segmentation - separating foreground from the background - is a key optimization, because it let's us zero out background samples which will lead to large size savings during the compression stage. I perform this step with an off the shelf library implementing various machine learning models. After some testing I've found out that segmentation at half-resolution and full resolution looks identical (the library is significantly downsampling my inputs anyway) and that sRGB-encoded pixels result in the highest quality, with linear-encoded pixels delivering worse quality and raw CFA samples (either all, or from individual channels) resulted in the lowest quality segmentation. ![Segmentation applied to RGB pixels](images/seg_mask.png width="340px") ![Segmentation applied to CFA samples](images/seg_bayered.jpg width="340px") Such segmentation is sensitive to the foreground - it might miss out animals, or props - depending on the categories that the model has been trained on. This can be a limitation in some industries, but can be overcome by training a custom segmentation model on an application-specific dataset, e.g. by using projected previous unsegmented reconstructions as ground truth labels. ## Mask post-processing Before masking our CFA samples by the segmentation mask it's necessary to process our mask slightly to reduce the probability of loosing foreground by inacurrate segmentation. Segmentation boundaries sometimes "leak" into the body, e.g. in motion-blurred areas. This process is relatively simple and computationally inexpensive. In this case I threshold the segmentation to get a binary mask, dilate it by a set number of iterations and then upsample to match the source resolution. The number of dilations provides me with a control of confidence versus resulting size: a low number results in a tight mask and smaller resulting file sizes and a larger number results in a higher confidence that none of the foreground has been missed out at the expense of larger resulting file sizes. ![Segmentation](images/seg_mask.png width="340px") ![Post-processed mask](images/seg_mask_processed.png width="340px") ## Compressing Why, how, also test jpeg-only compression, compare.
