**Efficient multi view data storage** [ <- ](..) _2025-09-02_ Working with multi-view data - for example, volumetric video - is demanding on storage, and therefore expensive and slow. In this post I explore practical ways to reduce that cost. # TL;DR The preprocessing pipeline below can achieve an **18:1** compression ratio on typical volumetric-video source footage. That reduces hardware (or cloud storage) costs and increases processing throughput. The process is lossless where it matters (the captured subject) and is transparent to downstream applications. It can turn this sample dataset (39 cameras, 3 frames, 12 megapixels, raw DNG, **1 719 MiB** in total): ![](images/sheet_in_censored_lq.jpg width="500px") Into this dataset (raw compressed & segmented DNG, **92 MiB** in total): ![](images/sheet_out_censored_lq.jpg width="500px") Both datasets produce the same volumetric video after reconstruction, but the compressed version is much lighter to store and faster / cheaper to process. !!! note *The multi-view dataset is courtesy of [IO Industries](https://www.ioindustries.com target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"), used with permission, the face has been blurred for privacy* # What is multi-view data and what makes it challenging Many applications require multi-view data. In photogrammetry, that means multiple viewpoints captured at a single moment. In volumetric video, it means multiple viewpoints captured over time. This data is used to reconstruct 3D geometry (for games, VFX, 3D printing) or to render novel viewpoints (useful for volumetric video and immersive experiences). Capturing multi-view data usually requires specialized camera rigs, and the sheer volume of recorded data (often captured at the highest spatial and temporal resolution and bit depth) frequently becomes the bottleneck in the processing pipeline. Teams that store and process this data need expensive storage hardware and fast infrastructure, which creates a large capital expense when starting out. On the other hand, slow processing increases operating expenses, especially when using cloud storage and processing. ![An example dataset: a 24fps second would take 13.4 GiB, a minute would take 806 GiB](images/sheet_in_censored_lq.jpg width="300px") # Workflow The key findings from this work are: - the [DNG specification](https://helpx.adobe.com/content/dam/help/en/photoshop/pdf/DNG_Spec_1_7_1_0.pdf target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer") supports several lossless compression methods, which alone can provide meaningful savings (up to about 2:1 in some cases), and - most of the recorded pixels are background that never contribute to the final reconstruction and can be safely removed prior to compression. Combining these two ideas reduces required storage by an order of magnitude. Now that we understand what multi-view data looks like and how much storage it requires, let's take a closer look at each step in the proposed workflow: ## Debayering Both the uncompressed input and the compressed output store raw Colour Filter Array samples (CFA - monochromatic sensor samples). However, for accurate segmentation we need a temporary colour image. We generate a temporary colour image by debayering, but only at half resolution and with modest quality. Because the colour image is used only for segmentation and discarded afterwards, half-resolution, low-cost debayering gives the same segmentation quality while being faster. ![Raw (CFA) samples](images/bayered.png width="340px") ![Debayered RGB pixels](images/debayered.png width="340px") ## Segmenting Segmentation - separating foreground from background - is the core optimization. Zeroing background CFA samples yields large savings in the subsequent lossless compression step. I run segmentation using an off-the-shelf library that implements several ML-based models. In my tests, half-resolution debayered images produced the same masks as full resolution (the library downscales inputs internally). I also observed that sRGB-encoded RGB inputs gave the best masks, linear-encoded RGB gave worse results, and raw CFA inputs (either combined or single-channel) produced the least accurate masks. Examples below show the difference in mask quality for different inputs: ![sRGB-encoded RGB pixels](images/seg_mask.png width="220px") ![Linearly-encoded RGB pixels](images/seg_linear.png width="220px") ![CFA samples](images/seg_bayered.jpg width="220px") Note: segmentation quality depends on the model's training data. It can miss unusual foreground content (animals, props, unusual clothing). This can be mitigated by training a domain-specific segmentation model using previously reconstructed frames as ground truth. ## Mask post-processing Before applying the segmentation mask to the CFA samples, we post-process the mask to reduce the risk of losing true foreground pixels. Segmentation boundaries can "leak" into motion-blurred or uncertain areas. The post-processing is simple and cheap: threshold the probability mask to a binary mask, dilate it a configurable number of iterations, then upsample to the source resolution. The dilation parameter trades off confidence versus compressed size: more dilation reduces the chance of clipped foreground at the cost of slightly larger files. ![Segmentation](images/seg_mask.png width="340px") ![Post-processed mask](images/seg_mask_processed.png width="340px") ## Compressing and writing The DNG format supports several compression schemes. For this pipeline I've tested lossless JPEG and lossless JPEG XL (distinct from their lossy counterparts). Both compress foreground and zeroed background effectively. JPEG XL gives the best ratio but is more computationally expensive. Keeping the data as CFA samples (rather than expanding to RGB) prevents file size inflation and preserves compatibility with downstream reconstruction tools - they continue to receive CFA samples exactly as before. To remain non-destructive and transparent, I copy all input DNG tags into the output files so that metadata and interpretation of CFA samples remain unchanged. ![Source CFA samples](images/cfa_src.png width="340px") ![Masked CFA samples ready for compression](images/cfa_masked.png width="340px") # How fast is it, how small can it get Now that the workflow is described, here are numbers that show the space savings and required compute time for the two compression schemes I tested: JPEG XL and JPEG. **JPEG XL** - Compression - Input size: 1 719 MiB - Compressed size: 92 MiB - Ratio: 18:1 - Time - 98 seconds for 117 images (≈ 0.84 s per image) - A single volumetric frame (70 x 12 megapixel cameras) would take ≈ minute to compress - A volumetric second (24 fps) would take ≈ 24 minutes to compress **JPEG** - Compression - Input size: 1 719 MiB - Compressed size: 374 MiB - Ratio: 4:1 - Time - 57 seconds for 117 images (≈ 0.49 s per image) - A single volumetric frame (70 x 12 megapixel cameras) would take ≈ 35 seconds to compress - A volumetric second (24 fps) would take ≈ 14 minutes to compress I ran these tests on a modest laptop (Intel i5, 8 x 2.4 GHz CPU, 16 GiB RAM, SSD). Profiling the JPEG XL run shows the relative contribution to total computation time as: debayering ~10%, segmenting ~30%, mask post-processing ~2%, compression ~58%. Memory use was similar for both compression schemes. Tunable parameters: - **Mask dilation iterations** (post-processing). More dilation reduces the risk of clipped foreground but slightly lowers the compression ratio. It has relatively little impact on compute time. - **JPEG XL parameters** (effort and decoding speed). After doing a parameter search I settled on `effort = 5` and `speed = 0` as a good compromise between compression ratio and CPU time. The [JPEG XL visualizer](https://jpegxl.info/resources/distance-vs-effort-visualizer.html target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer") is a useful reference when tuning these values. These parameters let you trade space vs. time vs. reliability. One optimization I did not test is running segmentation on a GPU, which could reduce overall processing time. I also tested JPEG XL without the segmentation step: **JPEG XL, without segmentation** - Compression - Input size: 1 719 MiB - Compressed size: 1 121 MiB - Ratio: 1.5:1 - Time - 80 seconds for 117 images (≈ 0.68 s per image) - A single volumetric frame (70 x 12 megapixel cameras) would take ≈ 50 seconds to compress - A volumetric second (24 fps) would take ≈ 20 minutes to compress This makes clear that segmentation is the key to achieving the high (18:1) compression ratios. Even without segmentation, however, lossless compression can still be useful in some edge cases. # Results Overall, the results exceeded my expectations. The pipeline produced a compression ratio of 18:1, which translates to lower storage costs, faster I/O, and cheaper downstream processing. I validated the output DNGs with the Adobe DNG SDK: the files are valid according to the specification, metadata is preserved, and the compression is lossless for the retained samples. I verified this by reading back written samples and comparing them to the input (and confirming that debayered foreground matches between uncompressed and compressed images). Any application using a recent Adobe DNG SDK should accept the compressed raw DNGs without changes - just as it accepts the uncompressed files. # Limitations There are a few caveats to keep in mind. - **Extra processing step.** The pipeline adds CPU work (compressing, segmentating, debayering and post-processing). That increases processing cost, but the process is highly parallelizable: adding more machines nearly linearly reduces processing time because each machine can process a different subset of images. - **Segmentation accuracy.** Off-the-shelf segmentation models can fail on uncommon foreground content (certain props, animals, or costumes). Workarounds include training a domain-specific segmentation model or disabling segmentation for problematic captures (you still get the benefit of lossless compression in that case, although with a lower ratio). - **Operational complexity.** I recommend a two-tier storage strategy: keep the original uncompressed DNGs in cold (cheap, slow, large) storage, and use the compressed files in hot (fast, expensive, small) storage for processing. If segmentation turns out to be incorrect for some captures, you can fall back to the original files. This approach reduces risk but does add file-management complexity. Whether this pipeline is worth integrating depends on your capture content and how reliable your segmentation is for that content. For many multi-view workflows, though, the storage and I/O savings make the extra processing step a worthwhile tradeoff.
